GitHub holds millions of projects that anyone can read and often improve. Software that anyone may read, use and change is called **open source**. In this lesson you will learn the three things you do to take part: reading and writing issues, copying a project with a fork, and sending your own change back. At the end you will know how to ask for a fix in someone else's project and how to find a project worth following.

## What you will do

1. Open a well-known open-source project on GitHub and find its **Issues** tab. An **issue** is a written note about a problem or an idea. Read two or three, and notice how good ones describe what happened and what the person expected.
2. Write an issue in your own practice repository, such as "The README has no setup steps." Ask your AI helper to improve it, then check that it is accurate and polite.
3. Learn what a **fork** is: your own copy of someone else's repository on GitHub, where you may make changes even though you cannot change the original. Fork a small practice project that the course points you to, or any project that welcomes beginners.
4. Make a small change on a branch in your fork, such as fixing a typo, and open a pull request to the original project, as you did in the last lesson. The owners decide whether to accept it.
5. Read the project's licence and its contribution guide before you contribute. Ask your AI helper to explain them in plain words.

**Safety note:** be kind and patient. Maintainers are often volunteers. Do not post personal information or secrets in an issue, because most issues are public.

## Check yourself

- You wrote one clear issue.
- You have a fork of a project and a branch with a small change in it.
- You can say what the licence of one project lets you do.
